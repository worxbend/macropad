Twelve keys. Two knobs. One line of light.

A 90 mm square macropad with hot-swap keys, an always-on screen and a glow that lives in the seam of the case. Board, case and firmware are all generated from code, all in one repository.

See the design Open the repository

Design stage. Nothing has been built, printed, milled or switched on yet. Every picture on this page is a render or a simulator frame, not a photo.

Everything a desk wants, in 90 millimetres.

Tactile. Hot-swappable. Per-key wired. Zero ghosting. Always-on. Sunlight-readable. Async Rust. Precision-milled option. Generated from code. And one thin line of light.

One pin per key, so the chip reads any combination of keys without ghosting; the computer receives up to six at once. A memory LCD that holds its picture without a backlight. Every key scanned once a millisecond, and firmware updates by dragging one file onto a drive. The milled case is drawn for a CNC shop; none has been cut yet. LED current is capped at 200 mA, a watchdog restarts the pad if the scan loop ever stalls, and six screws hide under the foot strips and the centre pad.

One slab. One seam.

A flat 90 × 90 mm top over a darker base, 17.5 mm tall, with nothing printed on its face. The two shells meet in a shadow gap that runs round the case and stops just short of the USB port. That gap is the line the case is drawn around, and it is where the light comes out.

From above it is a square with one job per zone. Knobs and screen along the back, a four by three grid of keys in a shallow dish, and the same 4.275 mm frame on the left, the right and the front.

Lights out. Line on.

Eight side-firing RGB LEDs sit on the board's left and right edges, four a side. A translucent bar carries their light to a slit in the seam, so the pad glows along one thin line on each side instead of from a strip.

  • Press a key and a ring of light runs outward from it.
  • Turn a knob and a dot of light follows it round the pad.
  • Change layer and the colour cross-fades in 300 ms.
  • Turn caps lock on and the back corners go white.
  • Let the computer sleep and every LED goes dark.

Current is capped at 200 mA, and dimming comes after the cap, so no setting can exceed it.

Print it. Or mill it.

One parametric model, two cases. Print it in PLA or PETG, or send the CNC variant to a machine shop for aluminium with continuous-curvature corners. The knobs are printed either way.

Take it apart.

Two knobs. Twelve keycaps on twelve MX switches. A glass lens and the top shell. The board. The base with its two light bars. Then six screws, two foot strips and a centre pad that hides the last two. Tape holds the lens; tape or glue holds the feet.

Cut through the middle.

The case is checked like a machined part. Every part is tested against every other part and every bought component, every named clearance is measured, and the printed variant is simulated layer by layer for overhangs and bridges.

659 part pairs per variant, 0 clashes, every named clearance inside its limit. Checked by the model, not by a print.

A real circuit board design. Every key on its own pin.

An RP2354A (the RP2350 chip of the Raspberry Pi Pico 2, with 2 MB of flash in the package) on a four-layer board. Twelve keys on twelve GPIOs: no matrix, no diodes, no ghosting. Hot-swap sockets take any MX-style switch without soldering. Code in the repository places the parts and routes the board with its own router, and KiCad's design-rule check compares the finished board with the schematic: zero differences.

nice!view, 160 × 68 memory LCD

Simulator frames. The firmware builds and passes its tests; it has never run on a chip.

The nice!view is a 160 × 68 memory LCD: always on and readable in sunlight. It shows the active layer, every key's label, both knob functions, caps and num lock, and a live typing-rate graph. Async Rust on embassy scans every key once a millisecond, and the same code draws these screens in a simulator on a PC.

Made for the work you repeat.

Twelve keys and two knobs, each doing something different on every layer. Here is what they do for the people who use them, with the stock keymap or a line or two of your own.

Create

Step frames with a knob.

Video editing, EDIT layer

Undo, redo, cut, copy and paste under one hand. Turn the left knob to walk back through undo history. Turn the right one to send Left and Right arrow, which many editors use to step one frame.

Eight keys your keyboard never had.

Streaming, MEDIA layer

F13 to F20, ready for scenes, sources and a “be right back” screen in your streaming software's hotkey settings. On this layer volume stays on the left knob, and the right knob's press mutes the computer's sound.

Your music software's shortcuts, one press each.

Music production, your keymap

Put the shortcuts your music software already has (transport, record, loop, markers) on keys, and keep system volume on a knob. It sends keystrokes, not MIDI.

Zoom on a dial.

Design tools, your keymap

Zoom on one knob (the keymap guide shows exactly that) and undo history on the other. The twelve keys hold the tools you switch between most.

Work

Save, find, select the line.

Coding and IDEs, EDIT and NAV layers

SAVE, FIND, and a LINE key that selects the whole line in one press. NAV adds Home, End, Page Up and Page Down, with tabs on a knob. Want a command palette or a build key? One chord() line in the keymap.

Mute the call in one press.

Office and meetings, chord() in the keymap

Put your call app's mute shortcut on one key and it is a single press away, labelled on the screen. On the MEDIA layer the right knob's press mutes the speakers.

Digits where your hand already is.

Number entry, NUM layer

Digits laid out like a keypad, with Backspace and Enter. They send the number-row keys, so Num Lock does not matter. Minus and plus are on a knob (US layout) and a decimal point is on its press.

Step, flip tabs, go back.

Navigation and reading, NAV layer

Step up and down with one knob (arrow keys), flip tabs with the other, Back and Forward on keys. The keymap guide includes a complete browser layer.

Everyday

Play, skip, turn it up.

Media control, MEDIA layer

Previous, play, next and stop on the top row. Volume on the left knob, screen brightness on the right, mute on its press.

Everything within one hand.

One-handed and accessible set-ups, tap_hold() and sequence()

Twelve keys within one hand's reach. Tap-hold gives one key two jobs, a macro types up to 16 strokes from one press, and the hot-swap sockets take whichever MX switch feels right. The optional 6° bar tilts it towards you.

F13 runs your script.

Home lab and smart home, F13 to F20

The pad only speaks USB, so the computer does the rest. Bind F13 to F20 in a shortcut tool on the computer to run a script, switch a scene in your home-automation system or restart a lab service. No wireless, no network and no cloud on the pad itself.

Signature Edition

Turn the milled case over. Beside the centre pad, a large O and then "leksandr B" in one joined stroke spell the designer's name. A 0.3 mm laser line, 34 mm wide, drawn for the anodised base from the same file that draws it here. Nothing has been marked yet.

This page draws the signature from cad/macropad_case/signature.py every time the site is built. The laser file comes from the same source when the case is built, so the two match. Nothing has been marked yet: what you see is the artwork and a render of where it goes. The artwork as SVG

Still open

  • The crystal is the reference part, but its oscillator is not measured. If USB does not start, look there first.
  • The inductor's polarity dot and every part's rotation must be checked in the board house's placement preview.
  • The pin orders of the display header and the side LEDs are still to be measured.
  • Every fit in the case is a guess until one print is measured.
  • How bright and even the light line is has not been simulated or tried.
